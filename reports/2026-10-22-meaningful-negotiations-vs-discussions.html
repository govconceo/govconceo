"Meaningful Negotiations" vs. "Discussions": What the FAR Part 15 Rewrite Changes in Competitive Range Exchanges

Key takeaways

  • "Discussions" becomes "negotiation." FAR Case 2026-003 would revise Part 15 "by focusing on negotiation instead of discussions." It adds a definition: an exchange that gives the offeror a chance to revise its proposal.
  • The floor stays. The preamble says the rule "still requires a meaningful negotiation with each offeror in the competitive range." At a minimum, deficiencies and significant weaknesses must be addressed.
  • Equal rounds are no longer required. Proposed 15.204-2(b)(2) lets the contracting officer negotiate further with one offeror without doing so with others.
  • "Deficiency" gets narrower and more objective. It would mean a part of a proposal that "does not conform to a material term" of the RFP. A "combination of significant weaknesses" would drop out of the definition.
  • Clarifications widen. They could happen at any time from receipt of proposals through award, with one offeror only. They could address perceived deficiencies and adverse past performance, but not change price or material elements.
  • Much of this is already in use. The model Part 15 deviation on Acquisition.gov, issued Sep 30, 2025, already contains the "further negotiate" text.

Findings

Why the Council wants to change the word

The preamble to FAR Case 2026-003 says current discussions are "often limited to fixing problems in an offeror's proposal, instead of truly negotiating." It says agencies also "forgo simple clarifications to avoid inadvertently crossing the boundary into a discussion and creating a protest risk."

Its stated aim is to prompt contracting officers "to proactively bargain on terms and conditions, such as intellectual property rights, licensing agreements, pricing, and other material aspects of the proposal."

Current FAR: negotiations in a competition are "called discussions"

Today's FAR 15.306(d) defines negotiations as exchanges "undertaken with the intent of allowing the offeror to revise its proposal," which may include bargaining. It then adds a label for competitions.

Under 15.306(d)(1) and (3), discussions "must be conducted ... with each offeror within the competitive range." At a minimum, they cover deficiencies, significant weaknesses, and adverse past performance the offeror has not had a chance to address.

Proposed FAR: one "negotiation" floor, then discretion

Proposed 15.204-2(b) keeps a minimum for every offeror in the competitive range and adds flexibility above it:

  • Must negotiate with each. Contracting officers "must ... negotiate with each offeror within the competitive range."
  • Must cover the minimum. At a minimum, they "indicate to, or negotiate with, each offeror any deficiencies or significant weaknesses."
  • May go further with one. Further negotiation with one offeror "does not obligate contracting officers to have further negotiations with any other offerors."
  • Need not cover everything. Contracting officers "are not required to negotiate every area where the proposal could be improved."

Proposed 15.204-2(d)(2) still requires a common cut-off date, or an equal amount of time, for proposal revisions from offerors left in the competitive range.

"Deficiency" is rewritten to be testable

The current FAR 15.001 defines a deficiency as "a material failure of a proposal to meet a Government requirement or a combination of significant weaknesses" that increases risk "to an unacceptable level."

Proposed 15.001 would define it as "any part of a proposal that does not conform to a material term of a RFP." A material term is one that "affects price, quantity, quality, or delivery, or any prerequisites that the RFP requires to be met at the time of proposal submission."

The preamble says the current wording produces "subjective interpretations" and "protests and challenges" over where clarifications end and deficiencies begin.

Clarifications become a broader, one-sided tool

Proposed 15.001 defines a clarification as an exchange to resolve minor or administrative errors or clarify aspects of a proposal. It "does not result in an opportunity for offerors to submit a proposal revision."

Proposed 15.202(a)(2) sets its limits:

  • Scope. Ambiguities, "perceived deficiencies, weaknesses, errors, omissions, or mistakes," relevance of past performance, and adverse past performance not yet answered.
  • Limit. Requests must not "alter the cost/price or other material elements of the proposal or cure a material omission."
  • Timing. "At any time after receipt of proposals through contract award."
  • No parity. Clarifying with one offeror does not require clarifying with any other.

The competitive range can still be cut

Proposed 15.204-1(b) keeps the efficiency limit. The range may be limited to "the greatest number that will permit an efficient competition among the most highly evaluated proposals." It cites 10 U.S.C. 3303 and 41 U.S.C. 3703.

Proposed 15.204-2(c)(2) says a proposal "may be eliminated from the competitive range at any time" once the offeror is unlikely to win, with no further negotiation required.

The statute still says "discussions"

41 U.S.C. 3703(a)(1) allows award "after discussions with the offerors, provided that written or oral discussions have been conducted with all responsible offerors who submit proposals within the competitive range." The proposed rule keeps a required negotiation with each offeror in the range.

The proposed rule cites 41 U.S.C. 3703 and 10 U.S.C. 3303 in its competitive-range and award sections. How GAO and the Court of Federal Claims will read the new text alongside the statute is not addressed in the sources opened.

What it means for contractors

Bid strategy

  • Expect uneven rounds. A competitor may get an extra negotiation you do not. Your first proposal and your first negotiation response carry more weight.
  • Write to the material terms. Under the new definition, a nonconformance with a material term is a deficiency. Compliance matrices matter more.
  • Prepare to bargain. The preamble names IP rights, licensing and pricing as targets for negotiation.

Compliance cost

  • Faster clarification turnarounds. Clarifications can arrive at any time before award. Keep a proposal team on call.
  • Past performance responses. Adverse past performance would be handled through clarifications, so have your rebuttals ready early.

Execution

  • Check which text applies. Agencies using the model Part 15 deviation already work this way. The RFP's Part 15 references tell you which rules govern.
  • Debriefing questions. Ask whether further negotiations were held with other offerors.
  • Protest posture. The equal-treatment question shifts from "were rounds equal?" to "was the minimum met?"

Watch next

  • Final rule. No final rule for FAR Case 2026-003 had been published as of Oct 22, 2026.
  • Protest decisions. GAO and Court of Federal Claims decisions applying the deviation or final text were not reviewed for this report.
  • DFARS conforming changes. DoD Part 215 alignment was not found in the sources opened.
