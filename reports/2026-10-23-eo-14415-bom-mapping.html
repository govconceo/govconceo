EO 14415’s Bill-of-Materials Mandate: What Defense Suppliers Must Map, and Which Dates Are Fixed So Far

Key takeaways

  • Policy due January 16, 2027. EO 14415 gives the Secretary of War 180 days from July 20, 2026, to issue policy requiring primes and subcontractors at any tier to map critical supply chains from raw materials to end products. Implementing regulations follow within 90 days of the policy’s completion.
  • Not yet a contract term. A Federal Register searched on October 23, 2026 found no implementing rule, and no published policy or data item description for the order’s bill of materials was located. The mapping requirements are directions to the Department, not clauses in your contract.
  • Three things the rules must require. The order says the regulations must require an indentured BOM back to raw materials, written vetting of suppliers for financial, foreign-ownership and manufacturing risk, and a ban on covered material from unreliable foreign suppliers.
  • Short clocks after vetting. The rules must require notice of significant risks within 15 days of completing vetting, and a confidential corrective action plan within 45 days.
  • One upstream rule is already dated. From January 1, 2027, DFARS 252.225-7052 bars covered magnets, tantalum and tungsten that were mined, refined, separated, melted or produced in China, Russia, North Korea or Iran.

Findings

The order directs rules; it does not impose them yet

Executive Order 14415, “Securing America’s Defense Supply Chains and Ensuring Domestic Acquisition of Critical Materials,” is addressed to the Secretary of War. Section 3 tells the Secretary to develop policy within 180 days and to issue regulations within 90 days of completing it.

The policy covers “all Department of War acquisitions that support, implicate, or relate to United States national security, as determined by the Secretary.” Scope will therefore be set in the policy, not the order.

  • Small business. The regulations are to ensure small businesses, non-traditional defense companies and new entrants can comply “without being unduly burdened.”
  • Consistency. They must also be consistent with statutory domestic and allied sourcing requirements.

The indentured BOM goes back to the mine

The order defines the bill of materials it wants. It is broader than a parts list.

Section 7(b) adds that the indentured BOM includes data for maintenance planning, logistics, reliability and maintainability, system safety, cost, cataloging and in-service feedback. It “will require a standard format, content, and data” set by data item descriptions.

  • Software counts. The definition includes software, so the BOM is not limited to hardware.
  • Who can see it. The order allows disclosure of a BOM to support contractors advising the Government, if they protect proprietary information. Expect to negotiate that protection.

Vetting has three named risk categories

Section 3(b)(ii) requires written procedures to vet “all suppliers and subcontractors that support the critical supply chain,” using existing Department supply chain risk assessment procedures. Section 7 defines each category.

  • Financial. A supplier cannot generate revenue to meet its obligations, which can lead to failure to perform, hostile takeover or bankruptcy.
  • Foreign ownership, control or influence. A foreign interest can direct or decide company matters in a way that may allow unauthorized access to information or harm national-security contracts.
  • Manufacturing and supply. A single supplier, sector or market cannot meet demand, including capacity limits, obsolescence, sole-source dependence and over-reliance on one source.
  • Critical supply chain. Section 7(a) defines it as all tiers providing anything essential to deliverables, mission assurance, security or resilience, “as defined by the Secretary.”

After vetting, the clocks start

Section 3(c) lists what the regulations must require once vetting is complete. Mitigation ties to the Supply Chain Risk Management Plan required under contract data item DI-MGMT-82256A.

  • Mitigate. Implement timely mitigation actions and track them to closure.
  • 15 days. Notify the Department of significant supply chain risks.
  • 45 days. Submit a written, confidential corrective action plan with a strict timeline.
  • Closeout. Submit a closeout report when the plan is complete.

Unreliable foreign suppliers will be off limits

Section 3(b)(iii) tells the rules to bar covered material from an “unreliable foreign supplier” in contractors’ supply chains. Section 6 sets out exceptions, including for Project Vault, the strategic critical-minerals reserve.

Section 7(f) defines the term as any person under the foreign ownership, control or influence of a covered nation under 10 U.S.C. 4872(f)(2), or of a nation the Secretary designates. DFARS 252.225-7052, which implements 10 U.S.C. 4872, lists North Korea, China, Russia and Iran as covered countries.

  • Alternative sources. Section 4 requires regulatory action, by January 16, 2027, requiring contractors relying on unreliable foreign suppliers to qualify alternatives “as soon as possible.” Failure is grounds to consider suspending or terminating orders or declining options.

DFARS already requires an upstream view on January 1, 2027

One part of the mapping problem already has a fixed date in a clause. DFARS 252.225-7052 (MAY 2024) changes on January 1, 2027.

  • Covered materials. Samarium-cobalt and neodymium-iron-boron magnets, tantalum metals and alloys, tungsten metal powder, and tungsten heavy alloy.
  • Whole chain. For both magnet types, tantalum and tungsten, the restriction reaches back to mining or ore and feedstock, including recycled material.
  • COTS narrows. From 2027, the COTS exception excludes items that are 50% or more covered material by weight.
  • Flowdown. The clause flows to subcontracts for items containing covered material, including commercial ones, unless an exception applies.

Waivers tighten at the same time

Section 2 of the order stops new 10 U.S.C. 4872(c)(1) waivers on January 1, 2027, unless the contractor submits a mitigation plan the Secretary accepts. Failing to qualify a domestic source is not non-availability unless efforts to qualify one are active, adequately funded and ongoing.

What it means for contractors

Bid strategy

Treat BOM depth as a future discriminator. When the rules arrive, suppliers who can already trace to raw materials will be cheaper to team with.

  • Covered materials first. If your products contain magnets, tantalum or tungsten, your January 1, 2027 DFARS obligations already need mine-level origin data.
  • Teaming terms. Write supply-chain data cooperation into teaming agreements and subcontracts now, before the rules make it mandatory.

Compliance cost

The cost driver is data from sub-tier suppliers who have never been asked for it. An indentured BOM to raw materials is a multi-tier data exercise, not a single report.

  • Format risk. The order says the BOM will follow standard data item descriptions. Build data in a structured form so it can be mapped to the format when published.
  • Vetting program. Financial, foreign-ownership and capacity screening will need written procedures. Existing DoD supply chain risk processes are the stated baseline.

Execution

Use the time before the policy to map the highest-risk paths. Start with covered materials, single-source parts and suppliers with any foreign-ownership questions.

  • Mitigation plans. If you rely on a 4872 waiver, prepare the four-part mitigation plan before January 1, 2027.
  • Track the policy. The 15- and 45-day clocks will start from completion of vetting, so know when your vetting will finish.

Watch next

  • Jan 1, 2027. DFARS covered-materials restriction expands upstream, and the waiver restrictions in EO 14415 begin.
  • Jan 16, 2027. Mapping policy and implementation guidance due, and regulatory action on alternative sources must be initiated.
  • Data item descriptions. Watch for the DID that will set the BOM’s standard format and content.
  • Implementing regulations. Due within 90 days after the policy is completed.
