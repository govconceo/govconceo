Post-Quantum Deadlines Are Set: 2030 for Key Exchange, 2031 for Signatures, and a FAR Rule Due by December 19

Key takeaways

  • A contractor rule is coming. Executive Order 14412 directs the FAR Council to propose, within 180 days of June 22, 2026 (by December 19, 2026), a rule requiring covered contractors to comply with NIST FIPS, including PQC standards, by December 31, 2030.
  • ”Covered contractor” is not yet defined. The order does not define the term. The proposed rule will decide who is in scope. A Federal Register searched on October 29, 2026 found no such proposed rule yet.
  • Agency deadlines drive purchases now. High value assets and high impact systems must use PQC for key establishment by December 31, 2030, and for digital signatures by December 31, 2031. National security systems are excluded from this part of the order.
  • Buy-side rules already apply. OMB M-26-15 tells agencies that product requirements in CISA’s listed categories should include PQC integration. CISA says agencies should buy only PQC-capable products in categories where they are widely available.
  • NIST set the sunset. NIST IR 8547 (final, October 2026) deprecates quantum-vulnerable public-key cryptography after 2030 and disallows it after 2035.

Findings

The order puts contractors on the same 2030 clock

EO 14412, “Securing the Nation Against Advanced Cryptographic Attacks,” has two procurement directives aimed at contractors. Both direct proposed rules, not final ones, so neither is yet a contract term.

  • Second rule. Section 6(d) directs a proposed rule within 270 days on contractor vulnerability disclosure programs. Programs would have to accept reports of cryptographic vulnerabilities, including testing for lack of encryption and use of non-FIPS approved algorithms.
  • Open scope question. Section 2 of the order defines terms such as “high impact system” and “key establishment,” but not “covered contractor.”

Agencies face hard dates for their most sensitive systems

Section 4(b) told OMB to require agencies to move high value assets and high impact systems to PQC key establishment by December 31, 2030. Digital signatures follow by December 31, 2031. National security systems are excluded from this directive.

OMB’s M-26-15 of June 24, 2026, implements that. It sets the objective of mitigating “as much quantum risk as feasible” by December 31, 2030, and does not apply to national security systems.

  • Phase 1, 2026–2027. Inventory, assessment and strategy.
  • Phase 2, 2027–2028. Pilots and early migration.
  • Phase 3, 2028–2030. PQC key establishment for HVAs, high impact systems and highly sensitive data.
  • Phase 4, 2031. PQC digital signatures for the same systems.

Vendors are written into the agency playbook

M-26-15 has a section on vendor and third-party software. It tells agencies to use CISA’s PQC product-categories list and to ensure that requirements for products in those categories include PQC integration.

It also tells agencies to engage FedRAMP cloud providers on PQC duties within the shared responsibility model. CISA and the Department of War, with GSA, will lead PQC migration for cloud services used by more than one agency.

  • Requirement owners. Appendix B gives the program office or requirement owner the role that “Ensures vendor requirements include PQC-readiness and cryptographic agility provisions.”
  • Third parties. Agency plans must include a third-party coordination plan and a plan for crypto-agile architecture.

CISA’s list tells agencies what to stop buying

CISA published its product-categories list on January 23, 2026, under Executive Order 14306. The list separates categories where PQC-capable products are widely available from categories still transitioning.

  • Widely available. Cloud PaaS and IaaS, chat and messaging, web browsers and servers, and data-at-rest and full-disk encryption.
  • Caveat. CISA notes that most of these categories have implemented PQC for key exchange but not yet widely for digital signatures, so they are not fully quantum-resistant.
  • Transitioning. Networking hardware and software, SaaS, telecommunications hardware, operating systems, identity and PKI software, and HSMs and smart cards, among others.

NIST fixed which algorithms survive

FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) were published August 13, 2024. M-26-15 identifies them as the standards for key establishment and digital signatures.

NIST IR 8547, final in October 2026, sets the retirement schedule. It follows an initial public draft issued in November 2024.

  • Public-key cryptography. RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA and other quantum-vulnerable public-key schemes are deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035.
  • Symmetric. NIST’s few symmetric standards at the 112-bit level will be disallowed in 2030.
  • SHA-1. It will be disallowed in FIPS-validated cryptographic modules after 2030.
  • Earlier dates still win. NIST says the 2030–2035 window does not change the earlier EO 14412 deadlines for HVAs and high impact systems.

A readiness checklist grounded in the official documents

The checklist below is built from what M-26-15 asks agencies to plan for and what EO 14412 tells the FAR Council to propose. Expect agency customers to ask vendors for the same evidence.

  • Inventory. Keep an automated, continuously updated inventory of the cryptography in your products and services. M-26-15 names software composition analysis of SBOMs, code scanning and network scanning as methods.
  • Cryptographic bill of materials. Be ready to export a CBOM. CISA guidance on its minimum elements is due by March 19, 2027.
  • Algorithm map. Flag RSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, DH and ECDH uses, plus SHA-1 and 112-bit symmetric uses, against the NIST IR 8547 dates.
  • TLS 1.3. Support TLS 1.3 or a successor. Agencies must support it by January 2, 2030.
  • FIPS validation. Plan validated modules that implement FIPS 203, 204 and 205. EO 14412 directs NIST to revise the validation program to speed up module validations.
  • Crypto agility. Make algorithms swappable by configuration, since M-26-15 asks agencies to ensure all systems are cryptographically agile.
  • Shared responsibility. If you hold a FedRAMP authorization, document who migrates what.
  • Disclosure program. Make sure your vulnerability disclosure policy can take reports of weak or missing encryption, ahead of the Section 6(d) rule.

What it means for contractors

Bid strategy

PQC capability is becoming a requirement in its own right for products in CISA’s widely-available categories. If you sell in those categories without PQC key exchange, expect to lose requirements-based evaluations.

  • Roadmap as evidence. For transitioning categories, a dated roadmap to FIPS 203 and 204 support answers M-26-15’s crypto-agility and PQC-readiness provisions.
  • Long contracts. Any contract whose performance runs past December 31, 2030, sits across the proposed contractor compliance date.

Compliance cost

The main cost is discovery: finding every place your product uses public-key cryptography, including in third-party libraries and firmware. Validation lead times add schedule risk.

  • Hardware lags. HSMs, smart cards, network gear and telecom hardware are on CISA’s transitioning list, not the widely-available list. Replacement cycles there are the long pole.

Execution

Watch the FAR Council’s December deadline. The proposed rule will settle who counts as a covered contractor, whether there is a flowdown and how compliance is shown.

  • Comment. A proposed rule will carry a comment period. Firms with legacy cryptography in long-lived systems should be ready to document cost and feasibility.

Watch next

  • Dec 19, 2026. 180-day deadline for the FAR proposed rule on FIPS and PQC compliance, and for NIST’s revision of the cryptographic module validation process.
  • Mar 19, 2027. 270-day deadline for the FAR vulnerability-disclosure proposed rule and CISA’s cryptographic bill of materials guidance.
  • CISA list updates. CISA says it will periodically move categories from the transitioning list to the widely-available list.
