No Federal Rule Bans AI-Written Bids. What the Official Record Says About Using AI on Proposals

Key takeaways

  • The FAR is silent. The current FAR on Acquisition.gov (FAC 2026-01) does not use the term “artificial intelligence” in Parts 4, 9, 12, 15, 25, 27, 39, 44 or 52. The DFARS parts reviewed (225, 227, 252) do not use it either. No government-wide rule permits or prohibits AI-drafted proposals.
  • Rules come solicitation by solicitation. OMB M-25-22 tells agencies to decide whether a solicitation should require disclosure of AI use in contract performance. Check Sections L and M and the instructions of every solicitation.
  • NASA asks for disclosure. NASA’s unsolicited-proposal guidebook, revised September 2026, requires proposers to say whether the work involves AI or machine learning, including generative AI. It warns against putting restricted proposal content into public AI tools.
  • GAO is warning about fabricated citations. In Bramstedt Surgical (B-424064, January 28, 2026), GAO said inaccurate case citations bore “the hallmarks” of unverified AI use. It warned that future filings with non-existent citations may lead to sanctions.
  • Grants differ from contracts. NIH, a grants program rather than a FAR procurement, will not treat applications substantially developed by AI as the applicant’s original ideas.

Findings

No FAR or DFARS text addresses AI-drafted proposals

A text search of the FAR parts that govern proposals, responsibility and contract clauses returned no use of the term “artificial intelligence.” The same was true of the DFARS parts reviewed and of the two Revolutionary FAR Overhaul proposed rules reviewed for this report.

That means no regulation tells offerors they may, or may not, use AI to write a bid. The existing rules on the accuracy of what an offeror submits apply regardless of the drafting tool.

  • What we searched. FAR Parts 4, 9, 12, 15, 25, 27, 39, 44 and 52 (FAC 2026-01), DFARS Parts 225, 227 and 252, and RFO proposed rules FAR Case 2026-011 (Parts 9, 27, 47) and the Parts 1, 2, 4, 33, 39, 40 and 53 rule.
  • What we did not search. Every agency FAR supplement, and individual solicitations. Agency-specific instructions may exist that this report did not find.

OMB left disclosure to each agency and solicitation

The OMB acquisition memo addresses contractor AI use, but in performance rather than in proposal writing. It excludes AI “used incidentally by a contractor during performance of a contract” from its scope, then asks agencies to consider whether disclosure is needed.

M-25-22 says agencies “must be cognizant of the risks posed by the unsolicited use of AI systems by vendors.” They must decide whether a solicitation should include “a provision in a solicitation requiring disclosure of AI use as part of any given contract’s performance.”

  • M-26-04. OMB’s LLM memo also excludes a contractor’s incidental use of AI for administrative purposes.
  • GSA clause. GSAR 552.239-7001 does not apply when LLM use is limited to a contractor’s internal or back-office systems that the Government does not receive or access.

NASA is the clearest agency statement found

NASA’s guidebook for unsolicited proposals, revised September 2026, has a section titled “Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning.” Proposers must say whether the submission involves developing, training, procuring or using AI or machine learning, “whether as a primary capability or incidental to the proposed work.”

If so, they must briefly describe intended use, safeguards, human oversight, data protection, validation and responsible-AI considerations. A boxed note then speaks to generative AI in proposal preparation.

  • Protect restricted content. The guidebook says not to enter proprietary, export-controlled, classified, sensitive or otherwise restricted proposal information into a public generative-AI service unless authorized.
  • More may follow. It adds that program-specific NASA guidance may impose additional disclosure or citation requirements for generative-AI help.

GAO is policing AI errors in protests

GAO decisions show where unchecked AI output has already caused problems: protest filings. In Bramstedt Surgical, the protester cited GAO decisions that did not exist or did not say what was claimed.

GAO dismissed the protest on other grounds and did not impose sanctions. It warned the firm that future filings citing non-existent authority may lead to sanctions.

  • Not the first. The decision cites earlier GAO decisions on the same issue, including Oready, LLC (B-423524.2, August 13, 2025), KE Systems Services (B-423881, December 22, 2025) and Raven Investigations & Security Consulting (B-423447, May 7, 2025).
  • Not anti-AI. GAO quoted earlier precedent that there is “nothing inherently wrong” with competently using AI, provided output is supervised and checked.

NIH shows a stricter model, for grants

NIH’s notice, published at 90 FR 37531 on August 5, 2025, covers research grant applications, not FAR contracts. It is the most restrictive federal statement on AI-written submissions found for this report.

NIH “will not consider applications that are either substantially developed by AI, or contain sections substantially developed by AI, to be original ideas of applicants.” If AI use is detected after award, NIH may refer the matter to the Office of Research Integrity and take enforcement action.

  • Application cap. The same notice limits each principal investigator to six new, renewal, resubmission or revision applications per calendar year.
  • Effective date. It applies to applications submitted for the September 25, 2025 receipt date and later.

What it means for contractors

Bid strategy

Read each solicitation’s instructions for AI disclosure or certification language, since that is where any rule will be. If there is none, no FAR rule found requires you to disclose that AI helped draft the proposal.

  • Proposed work vs. drafting. NASA’s disclosure covers AI in the proposed work, incidental or primary. Do not confuse it with disclosure of drafting tools unless a program says so.
  • Grants are different. If you also pursue NIH funding, its originality policy limits AI-drafted applications in ways no FAR rule does.

Compliance cost

The main cost is review time. Every fact, figure, past-performance claim and citation produced with AI needs a human check before submission.

  • Tool controls. Use AI tools that do not train on or retain your inputs for proposal content. NASA’s warning covers proprietary, export-controlled and other restricted information.
  • Teaming data. Partner pricing and technical data shared under NDAs should get the same restrictions.

Execution

Carry the same discipline into protests and claims. GAO has now flagged AI-style citation errors in several decisions and has reserved the right to impose sanctions.

  • Citation check. Verify every GAO B-number, party name and date against gao.gov before filing.
  • Performance disclosure. After award, check whether the contract requires disclosure of AI use in performance, as M-25-22 invites agencies to require.

Watch next

  • Agency solicitation language. Watch for AI disclosure provisions in agency solicitations and supplements that follow M-25-22.
  • Other NASA programs. The guidebook notes that program-specific guidance may add disclosure or citation rules for generative AI.
  • GAO sanctions. GAO has warned but, in the decision reviewed, did not impose sanctions. A sanctions decision would raise the stakes.
