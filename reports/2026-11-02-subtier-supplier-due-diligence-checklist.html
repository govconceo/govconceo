Vetting Sub-Tier Suppliers: A Prime Contractor’s Checklist Built From the Clauses That Already Apply

Key takeaways

  • The prime owns subcontractor responsibility. FAR 9.104-4 says prospective primes are generally responsible for determining the responsibility of their prospective subcontractors. A weak subcontractor can affect the Government’s view of the prime.
  • Exclusions check above $45,000. FAR 52.209-6 bars subcontracts above $45,000 with debarred, suspended or proposed-for-debarment firms, other than for COTS items, absent a compelling reason. It requires written disclosure from each such subcontractor.
  • Security bans flow to commercial subs. FAR 52.244-6 makes primes flow down Kaspersky, Section 889, ByteDance and FASCSA clauses even in commercial subcontracts. FAR 52.204-21 safeguarding also flows down where subcontractors hold federal contract information, other than for COTS items.
  • DoD adds more. DFARS clauses add flowdowns for covered defense information, CMMC status, electronic-parts sourcing and covered materials, each with its own trigger.
  • More is coming. EO 14415 will require written vetting for financial, foreign-ownership and manufacturing risk on defense supply chains. Its policy is due January 16, 2027.

Findings

Responsibility starts with the prime

The FAR places the first duty to vet subcontractors on the prime. That duty predates every supply-chain clause below.

FAR 9.104-4 adds that a subcontractor’s responsibility “may affect the Government’s determination of the prospective prime contractor’s responsibility.” A prime may be asked for written evidence of a proposed subcontractor’s responsibility.

Check 1: exclusions before award

FAR 52.209-6 (Jan 2025) covers subcontracts above the FAR 9.405-2(b) threshold, currently $45,000, other than for COTS items. Three duties apply.

  • No excluded subs. Do not subcontract with a firm that is debarred, suspended or proposed for debarment, unless there is a compelling reason.
  • Written disclosure. Require each covered subcontractor to disclose in writing whether it or its principals are debarred, suspended, proposed for debarment or voluntarily excluded.
  • Notify the CO. A corporate officer or designee must notify the contracting officer in writing before subcontracting with an excluded party.

Check 2: prohibited sources and products

Four FAR security prohibitions must be flowed down even in commercial subcontracts under FAR 52.244-6. Each needs a supplier-level check, not just a clause in the purchase order.

  • 52.204-23, Kaspersky. Hardware, software and services from Kaspersky Lab covered entities.
  • 52.204-25, Section 889. Huawei, ZTE and, for specified security purposes, Hytera, Hikvision and Dahua equipment and services. The flowdown excludes the use ban in (b)(2).
  • 52.204-27, ByteDance. The TikTok application or any successor.
  • 52.204-30, FASCSA. Products subject to FASCSA orders, currently the DHS orders on DeepSeek and Acronis. The flowdown excludes the quarterly SAM check, but subcontractors must report hits upward.

Check 3: cybersecurity flowdowns follow the data

FAR and DFARS cyber clauses flow down based on what information the subcontractor handles. Map each subcontractor’s data access first.

  • FAR 52.204-21. Flows to subcontracts, including commercial ones other than COTS, where federal contract information resides in or transits the subcontractor’s systems.
  • DFARS 252.204-7012(m). Flows to subcontracts for operationally critical support or involving covered defense information, including commercial ones. The prime decides whether information keeps its covered-defense-information status.
  • DFARS 252.204-7021(f). Where the clause is in your contract, flow it down and confirm subcontractors complete the required CMMC affirmation before award.

Check 4: parts and materials provenance

Two DFARS clauses require primes to know where physical parts and materials come from. Both reach commercial subcontracts.

  • Electronic parts. DFARS 252.246-7008 flows to subcontracts for electronic parts or assemblies containing them, unless the subcontractor is the original manufacturer. Parts from other sources trigger traceability or testing duties.
  • Covered materials. DFARS 252.225-7052 flows to subcontracts for items containing covered magnets, tantalum or tungsten. From January 1, 2027, the restriction reaches material mined, refined or separated in China, Russia, North Korea or Iran.
  • Domestic content. Under FAR 52.225-1, components of unknown origin are treated as foreign. Supplier origin data directly affects Buy American compliance.

Check 5: AI subcontractors on GSA work

On GSA orders where GSAR 552.239-7001 applies, the clause flows to subcontractors doing AI design, development, deployment, or operation and monitoring that handle Government Data. The prime must use best efforts to vet them.

  • Evidence. The clause accepts flowdown, attestation backed by verifiable evidence where practicable, and existing artifacts such as FedRAMP packages, SOC 2 reports or ISO/IEC 27001 certifications.
  • 72 hours. The prime must tell the contracting officer within 72 hours of actual knowledge of a material violation.

Check 6: prepare for EO 14415 vetting

EO 14415 tells the Secretary of War to require written procedures to vet “all suppliers and subcontractors that support the critical supply chain” in defense acquisitions. The regulations are not yet issued.

  • Financial. A supplier’s inability to meet its financial obligations.
  • Foreign ownership, control or influence. A foreign interest’s power to direct the company in ways that may expose information or harm national-security contracts.
  • Manufacturing and supply. Capacity limits, obsolescence, sole-source and concentration risk.
  • Clocks to expect. Notice of significant risks within 15 days of completing vetting, and a corrective action plan within 45 days.

What it means for contractors

Bid strategy

Supplier readiness now affects whether you can propose a team at all. A subcontractor that cannot answer exclusion, 889, FASCSA and cyber questions is a responsibility risk for the prime.

  • Pre-qualify. Collect disclosures and affirmations before you name subcontractors in a proposal, not after award.
  • Commercial subs. Do not assume commercial status removes flowdowns. FAR 52.244-6 lists the clauses that still apply.

Compliance cost

Most of the cost is building one supplier record that answers every clause. Separate spreadsheets for each rule produce gaps.

  • One record. For each supplier, keep exclusion checks, 889 and FASCSA certifications, data access, CMMC status, part sources and material origin.
  • Refresh cycle. Re-check exclusions at each subcontract award, and FASCSA orders at least quarterly.

Execution

Write reporting paths into subcontracts. Several clauses depend on subcontractors notifying you fast enough for you to meet one-business-day, 3-business-day or 72-hour clocks.

  • Notice terms. Require subcontractors to report covered telecom, FASCSA hits and cyber incidents to you promptly.
  • Records. Keep evidence of each check. Reasonable-inquiry standards depend on the records you hold.

Watch next

  • Jan 16, 2027. EO 14415 vetting and mapping policy due, followed by regulations within 90 days of its completion.
  • FAR overhaul. The proposed Part 40 rule would combine the security prohibitions into one clause with a 72-hour reporting clock and one reasonable-inquiry standard.
  • New FASCSA orders. Each new order adds a product to screen for across your supplier base.
