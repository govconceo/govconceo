Section 889 Due Diligence: The Representation, the One-Business-Day Report and What the FAR Overhaul Would Change

Key takeaways

  • Two prohibitions, both in force. Since August 13, 2019, contractors may not provide covered telecom to the Government. Since August 13, 2020, agencies may not contract with an entity that uses covered telecom as a substantial or essential component of any system, whether or not the use is on federal work.
  • Five named companies, plus any DoD designates. Huawei and ZTE, and for specified security purposes Hytera, Hikvision and Dahua, including subsidiaries and affiliates. The list also covers entities the Secretary of Defense designates.
  • Reasonable inquiry, not audit. The FAR defines the required check as an inquiry into information in your possession, excluding any need for an internal or third-party audit.
  • Report within one business day. If you find covered telecom during performance, report within one business day, then follow up within 10 business days. DoD contracts also carry DFARS 252.204-7018, with 3- and 30-business-day clocks.
  • Overhaul would simplify. A June 23, 2026 proposed rule would move Section 889 into a consolidated FAR Part 40 clause and set a single 72-hour report. It would also say commercial sales, maintenance, testing, warranty work and employees’ personal devices are not individually “use.”

Findings

The two prohibitions reach different things

FAR 52.204-25 implements Section 889 of the FY2019 National Defense Authorization Act. Paragraph (b)(1) bars providing covered telecom to the Government. Paragraph (b)(2) bars agencies from contracting with an entity that uses it.

Both prohibitions turn on use “as a substantial or essential component of any system, or as critical technology as part of any system.” A waiver under FAR 4.2104, or one of two exceptions, takes an item outside the clause.

  • Exception one. A service that connects to a third party’s facilities, such as backhaul, roaming or interconnection.
  • Exception two. Telecom equipment that cannot route or redirect user data or see the data it handles.

Who and what is covered

The FAR definition of “covered telecommunications equipment or services” has four parts. The second is limited by purpose, which matters for video cameras.

  • Huawei and ZTE. Telecommunications equipment they produce, and that of their subsidiaries or affiliates.
  • Hytera, Hikvision and Dahua. Video surveillance and telecom equipment they produce, for public safety, security of government facilities, physical surveillance of critical infrastructure and other national security purposes.
  • Services. Telecom or video surveillance services provided by those entities or using that equipment.
  • DoD designations. Equipment or services from entities the Secretary of Defense reasonably believes are owned or controlled by, or connected to, the government of a covered foreign country.

Representations happen at least once and often twice

FAR 52.204-26 is the representation in SAM. The offeror checks SAM’s excluded parties list for covered telecom entities, then represents whether it does or does not provide, and after a reasonable inquiry whether it does or does not use, covered telecom.

FAR 52.204-24 goes in every solicitation, and in notices or solicitations for orders under indefinite-delivery contracts. If the SAM representation is “does not,” the offeror need not complete the matching part of 52.204-24. If it is “does,” detailed disclosures are required.

  • Keep SAM current. The 52.204-26 answers feed every offer. An outdated “does not” becomes an inaccurate representation once your network changes.

Reasonable inquiry has a defined floor

FAR 52.204-25 defines a reasonable inquiry as one “designed to uncover any information in the entity’s possession” about who produced or provided its telecom. The definition expressly excludes the need for an internal or third-party audit.

That sets a floor, not a ceiling. The inquiry is about records you hold, so the quality of your asset inventory and purchasing records determines how good the answer is.

  • Where to look. Network and telecom asset registers, procurement records for cameras and radios, managed-service contracts, and supplier certifications.

Reporting clocks are short

If covered telecom is found during performance, through your own checks or a subcontractor’s notice, paragraph (d) of 52.204-25 sets two deadlines. DoD contractors report at dibnet.dod.mil; others report to the contracting officer.

  • One business day. Contract and order numbers, supplier name, UEI and CAGE if known, brand, model, description and any mitigation.
  • Ten business days. Further mitigation, the efforts made to prevent use, and planned efforts to prevent recurrence.
  • DoD clause too. DFARS 252.204-7018 requires a report at dibnet.dod.mil within 3 business days and further information within 30 business days for covered defense telecom.

Flowdown carries the provider ban, not the use ban

FAR 52.204-25(e) requires the clause in all subcontracts, including commercial ones, but excluding paragraph (b)(2). Subcontractors are bound by the ban on providing covered telecom, not by the ban on using it.

DFARS 252.204-7018 flows to all subcontracts, including commercial, without that carve-out. The prime’s own use remains the prime’s responsibility under (b)(2).

Agency waivers have run out

FAR 4.2104(a) allowed one-time agency waivers, for no longer than August 13, 2021, for the provide ban and August 13, 2022, for the use ban. Both dates have passed.

The FAR still provides an emergency procedure, and FAR 4.2104(b) provides a separate waiver authority for the Director of National Intelligence. Contractors should not plan on a waiver.

The FAR overhaul would narrow and simplify

The Revolutionary FAR Overhaul proposed rule (FAR Case 2026-001, 91 FR 37550, June 23, 2026) would consolidate Section 889 and other security prohibitions into FAR Part 40. It would use one provision, 52.240-2, and one clause, 52.240-3, “Security Prohibitions and Exclusions.”

  • Definitions. It would clarify what “produced” means and narrow “critical technology” to technology without which a system cannot adequately operate.
  • Reporting. It would standardize reporting at 72 hours from discovery, with one report.
  • Status. Comments closed July 23, 2026. The current FAR clauses govern until a final rule takes effect.

What it means for contractors

Bid strategy

Every offer relies on your 52.204-26 representation. Re-verify it before major bids, and after any acquisition, office move or network refresh.

  • Acquisitions. A target company’s cameras, radios or network gear become your “use” after closing. Make Section 889 a diligence item.
  • Video systems. Building security systems are a common source of Hikvision and Dahua equipment, including through resellers that rebrand it.

Compliance cost

The recurring cost is maintaining an inventory good enough to support the representation and a one-business-day report. The audit-free standard keeps the inquiry bounded.

  • Supplier certifications. Ask telecom, IT and security suppliers to certify against the 889 definition, and keep the records.
  • Managed services. Include 889 terms in managed network and cloud contracts, since their equipment can be your “use.”

Execution

Write a playbook for the one-business-day report before you need it. Know who files, where they file and what fields are required.

  • Flowdown check. Confirm 52.204-25 is in every subcontract and purchase order, including commercial ones, and DFARS 252.204-7018 for DoD work.
  • Track the overhaul. If finalized, the 72-hour clock and Part 40 clause will replace current procedures. Update the playbook then.

Watch next

  • Final Part 40 rule. Would replace 52.204-24, -25 and -26 with 52.240-2 and 52.240-3 and change the reporting clock.
  • DoD designations. Any new Secretary of Defense designation would add entities to the definition.
  • SAM list. Check the SAM excluded-parties list for covered telecom entities before each representation.
