Cyber Compliance Is False Claims Act Risk: 18 DOJ Cyber-Fraud Resolutions Since 2022, Totaling About $82.5 Million

Key takeaways

  • 18 resolutions, about $82.5 million. DOJ press releases opened for this report announce 18 cybersecurity False Claims Act resolutions from March 2022 to November 3, 2026. Together they total $82,521,106. This count may not be complete.
  • The pace is rising. Ten of the 18 were announced in calendar 2025 or 2026, against eight in 2022 through 2024. Nine of those ten involved DoD contracts or suppliers.
  • The theories repeat. Missing system security plans, unimplemented NIST SP 800-171 controls, inaccurate SPRS scores, and cloud providers that did not meet FedRAMP Moderate-equivalent rules.
  • Insiders bring the cases. Whistleblowers filed most of the defense cases. Shares range from $65,291 (Swiss Automation) to $2.61 million (Aerojet Rocketdyne).
  • Self-disclosure earns credit. DOJ gave cooperation credit to Verizon (2023), ASRC Federal (2024) and Aero Turbine and Gallant Capital (2025).
  • The statute bites hard. The FCA allows treble damages plus per-claim penalties of $14,308 to $28,619. "Knowingly" includes reckless disregard and needs no proof of intent to defraud.

Amounts are the settlement amounts DOJ announced. Except where a release says facts were admitted, they are allegations, and there has been no determination of liability.

The first case was not a DoD case

DOJ described the March 2022 Comprehensive Health Services settlement as its "first resolution of a False Claims Act case involving cyber fraud since the launch" of the initiative. The contracts were with the State Department and the Air Force for medical services in Iraq and Afghanistan.

Five of the next seven involved civilian or state-administered programs. They included Florida children's insurance, GSA internet services, Pennsylvania contact tracing and New York rental assistance. Cyber FCA risk is not limited to DFARS clauses.

Since 2025, the defense cases cluster around four failures

The defense resolutions from 2025 onward share recurring allegations:

  • No or stale SSP. MORSECORP (admitted), Raytheon/Nightwing and ILC Dover.
  • Unimplemented 800-171 controls. MORSECORP, LOGZONE, Honeywell and ILC Dover.
  • Score problems. MORSECORP's 104 vs -142, LOGZONE's DCMA score of -170, and ILC's lack of a current assessment.
  • Cloud providers. MORSECORP (email hosting) and ILC Dover (CUI storage), both without FedRAMP Moderate-equivalent security and 7012 incident duties.

Subcontractors and suppliers are in scope

The December 2025 Swiss Automation release described a precision machining business that supplies parts to DoD primes and subcontractors. DOJ alleged the company "knew" the 800-171 duty applied "not only to DoD prime contractors, but also to subcontractors and suppliers."

The November 2026 ILC Dover release quotes Assistant Attorney General Brett A. Shumate. He said contractors "must comply with the cybersecurity requirements in their federal contracts and subcontracts."

Whistleblowers drive the docket

DOJ releases report these relator shares:

  • Aerojet Rocketdyne. Brian Markus, $2.61 million.
  • ILC Dover. Philip Root, former Director of Cybersecurity, $2.35 million.
  • Illumina. Erica Lenore, $1,900,000.
  • Raytheon/Nightwing. A former Director of Engineering, $1,512,000.
  • MORSECORP. $851,000.
  • Honeywell Aerospace. Rachel Tenney, $375,823.
  • Penn State. Matthew Decker, former CIO of the Applied Research Laboratory, $250,000.
  • Georgia Tech. Christopher Craig and Kyle Koza, $201,250.
  • Swiss Automation. Jaime Gomez, former quality-control manager, $65,291.

DOJ's FY2025 statistics release (Jan 16, 2026) reported 1,297 qui tam suits, "the highest number in a single year." The prior record was 980 in 2024.

Cooperation is the one lever DOJ advertises

Three releases describe cooperation credit:

  • Verizon (2023). The company made a written self-disclosure, ran an independent investigation, and gave multiple supplemental disclosures.
  • ASRC Federal (2024). It promptly notified CMS of the breach and cooperated. It also waived at least $877,578 in remediation costs.
  • Aero Turbine and Gallant Capital (2025). DOJ said contractors "can mitigate the consequences by making timely self-disclosures, cooperating with investigations, and taking prompt remedial measures."

The Aero Turbine case also named a private equity owner, Gallant Capital Partners, as a settling party.

Why exposure scales fast

Under 31 U.S.C. 3729(a), a violator is liable for a civil penalty per claim plus three times the Government's damages. 28 CFR 85.5 sets that penalty at $14,308 to $28,619 per claim for penalties assessed after July 3, 2025.

Section 3729(b)(1) defines "knowingly" to include deliberate ignorance and reckless disregard, and to "require no proof of specific intent to defraud." Every invoice on a contract with an unmet cyber clause can be a claim.

What it means for contractors

Bid strategy

  • Your SPRS score is a representation. MORSECORP and LOGZONE show scores are compared against what assessors find.
  • Diligence the target. Gallant Capital's settlement shows owners can be parties. Cyber posture belongs in acquisition due diligence.
  • Primes should vet suppliers. Swiss Automation shows DOJ will pursue lower tiers directly.

Compliance cost

  • Fix and disclose beats discovered. The three cooperation cases are DOJ's stated model.
  • Fund the internal voice. Several relators were cybersecurity or IT leaders. A working escalation path reduces the chance a concern goes outside first.
  • Correct SPRS promptly. MORSECORP's 11-month delay after learning its real score was part of its admitted facts.

Execution

  • Inventory clouds. Two recent cases turned on third-party cloud or email hosting.
  • Keep the SSP current. It appears in at least three defense resolutions.
  • Track plan dates. Penn State's case was about dates in plans of action, not only the controls.

Watch next

  • New releases. ILC Dover (Nov 3, 2026) is the latest found. DOJ's Civil Division continues to announce resolutions.
  • CMMC. Phase 2 is suspended under the July 13, 2026 memo. Self-assessment affirmations under 252.204-7021 remain a representation where required.
  • FY2026 statistics. DOJ typically publishes annual FCA statistics in January or February. The FY2025 release came Jan 16, 2026.
