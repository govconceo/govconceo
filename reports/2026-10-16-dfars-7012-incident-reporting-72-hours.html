DFARS 7012 Incident Reporting: The 72-Hour Clock, the 90-Day Hold, and Day-One Actions

Key takeaways

  • 72 hours from discovery. DFARS 252.204-7012 defines "rapidly report" as "within 72 hours of discovery of any cyber incident." Reports go to DoD at dibnet.dod.mil.
  • You need a certificate first. Reporting requires a DoD-approved medium assurance certificate. Get one before an incident, not during it.
  • Preserve for 90 days. Images of affected systems and relevant monitoring and packet-capture data must be kept at least 90 days from the report.
  • Malware goes to DC3, not the contracting officer. The clause says so in plain terms.
  • A report is not an admission. DFARS 204.7302(d) says a reported incident "shall not, by itself" be read as evidence of inadequate security.
  • Subs report too. Under 7012(m), subcontractors must give the DoD-assigned incident report number to the prime or next higher tier.
  • A FAR-wide 72-hour rule is proposed. FAR Case 2026-001 would set 72 hours for CUI incidents governmentwide, with non-DoD reports going to CISA. The January 2025 version had proposed 8 hours. Neither is final.

Findings

What triggers the clock

DFARS 252.204-7012(c)(1) applies when the contractor "discovers a cyber incident" that affects any of three things:

  • A covered contractor information system.
  • The covered defense information residing on it.
  • Operationally critical support. This means the contractor's ability to perform contract requirements designated as operationally critical support.

The clause defines a cyber incident as actions through computer networks "that result in a compromise or an actual or potentially adverse effect on an information system and/or the information residing therein." "Compromise" includes unauthorized disclosure, modification, destruction or loss, and copying to unauthorized media where any of these "may have occurred."

Two duties run in parallel: review and report

Paragraph (c)(1) requires two actions:

  • Review for compromise. Identify compromised computers, servers, specific data and user accounts. The review must cover systems that were part of the incident and other systems on the network that may have been accessed.
  • Rapidly report. Report to DoD at https://dibnet.dod.mil.

Paragraph (c)(2) says the report is treated as information created by or for DoD. It must include, at a minimum, the elements required at dibnet.dod.mil.

The certificate is a day-zero item

Paragraph (c)(3) requires the contractor or subcontractor to "have or acquire" a DoD-approved medium assurance certificate to report. The clause points to https://public.cyber.mil/eca/ for how to obtain one.

Because the 72-hour clock runs from discovery, a company that first applies for a certificate after an incident risks missing the deadline. The clause does not provide extra time for that.

Malware, media and forensics

After the report, paragraphs (d) through (g) set out what DoD may ask for:

  • (d) Malicious software. Submit isolated malware to the DoD Cyber Crime Center (DC3) under DC3's or the contracting officer's instructions. "Do not send the malicious software to the Contracting Officer."
  • (e) Media preservation. Preserve images of all known affected systems and all relevant monitoring and packet-capture data for at least 90 days from the report, so DoD can request the media or decline interest.
  • (f) Forensic access. On request, give DoD access to additional information or equipment needed for forensic analysis.
  • (g) Damage assessment. If DoD elects to do a damage assessment, the contracting officer will request the information gathered under paragraph (e).

Cloud providers carry the same duties

Under 7012(b)(2)(ii)(D), a contractor using an external cloud provider for covered defense information must ensure the provider meets FedRAMP Moderate-equivalent security. The provider must also comply with paragraphs (c) through (g), covering incident reporting, malware, media preservation, forensic access and damage assessment.

Separately, DFARS 252.239-7010(d) requires reporting of all cyber incidents related to cloud computing services provided under that clause, also via DIBNet.

Subcontractors: report to DoD, tell the prime

DFARS 252.204-7012(m)(2)(ii) requires subcontractors to give the incident report number, "automatically assigned by DoD," to the prime or next higher-tier subcontractor "as soon as practicable." DFARS 204.7302(b) describes the same flow.

That gives the prime a trackable reference without the subcontractor having to share its full report up the chain.

Reporting is not proof of a violation

The same paragraph says the contracting officer must consult the DoD component CIO or cybersecurity office before assessing compliance. Incidents are considered "in the context of an overall assessment."

How DoD may use what you report

Paragraphs (i) and (j) authorize release of reported information outside DoD in defined cases. These include entities whose missions may be affected, entities assisting with diagnosis or mitigation, and counterintelligence or law enforcement investigations.

Paragraph (l) says 7012 "in no way abrogates" other reporting duties under other clauses or law. A 7012 report may not be the only notice an incident requires.

Other clocks that may run at the same time

  • Section 889 equipment. FAR 52.204-25(d) requires a report within one business day of identifying covered telecommunications equipment or services. For DoD it goes to dibnet.dod.mil.
  • Proposed FAR CUI clause. FAR Case 2026-001 (91 FR 37550, June 23, 2026) proposes clause 52.240-7. CUI incidents would be reported within 72 hours of discovery, to DIBNet for DoD and to CISA for other agencies, with notice to the contracting officer.
  • Earlier proposal. The January 15, 2025 FAR CUI proposed rule (90 FR 4278) had proposed 8 hours. The 2026 preamble says commenters' concerns led to the 72-hour change.

The 2026 preamble also proposes standardizing supply-chain prohibition disclosures at 72 hours. That would replace the one-business-day 889 report, if the rule is finalized.

What it means for contractors

Bid strategy

  • Show an incident plan. Proposals for CUI work can point to a tested 7012 process, including who holds the certificate.
  • Check cloud terms. Provider contracts need to pass through paragraphs (c) to (g).

Compliance cost

  • Storage. 90 days of images and packet capture for affected systems has a real storage cost. Size it in advance.
  • Certificates. Budget for medium assurance certificates for named reporters, with backups.
  • Retainers. Forensic support may be needed inside the 72-hour window.

Execution: day-one actions drawn from the clause

  1. Start the clock. Record the discovery time; the 72 hours run from it.
  2. Scope the review. Identify compromised computers, servers, data and accounts, and other systems that may have been accessed.
  3. Preserve. Capture images and monitoring/packet-capture data before remediation overwrites them; hold for at least 90 days from the report.
  4. Report at DIBNet. Use the medium assurance certificate and submit the minimum required elements.
  5. Isolate and send malware to DC3. Not to the contracting officer.
  6. Notify up the chain. Subcontractors pass the DoD report number to the prime.
  7. Check other clocks. Section 889, other contract clauses, and other laws under paragraph (l).

Watch next

  • Final FAR Part 40. If finalized, proposed 52.240-7 would add a governmentwide CUI incident rule alongside 7012. No final rule had been published as of Oct 16, 2026.
  • DFARS alignment. No DFARS rule conforming 7012 to the proposed FAR clause was found.
  • CMMC review. Phase 2 remains suspended under the July 13, 2026 memo. 7012 still applies.
